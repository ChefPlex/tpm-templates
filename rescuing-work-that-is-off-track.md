# Rescuing Work That Is Off Track

Most guidance for program managers assumes you are starting something. You get a charter, a kickoff, a clean backlog, and a set of people who have agreed to be there.

That is not the job most of the time. Most of the time somebody hands you work that's already moving, already late, and already the subject of an argument. It might be a program. It might be a team, a service, a vendor relationship, or a stream of requests that never had a shape in the first place. The label does not change the approach.

This is what to do in that situation, in the order to do it.

---

## The whole thing is six questions you already know

Before any of the mechanics, there is a frame worth keeping in your head, and you learned it writing reports as a kid.

**Who, what, when, where, why, and how.**

That is not a cute mnemonic. Work goes off track when the answers to those six questions stop agreeing across the people involved, and it comes back when they agree again. Everything below is a way of answering them and then getting everybody holding the same answers.

| Question | What it means here | Where it gets answered |
|---|---|---|
| **How** | How is it broken. The symptom you were called about. | Section 1 |
| **Why** | Why is it broken. Almost always a communication failure that hardened into an expectation. | Section 1 |
| **Where** | Where is it broken. The handoff, the team, the decision. This is what tells you who to talk to. | Section 1 |
| **Who** | Who is doing it, who is asking for it, who is funding it. | Section 2 |
| **What** | What actually needs to get done, as opposed to what people think is being done. | Section 3 |
| **When** | When it will realistically land, rebuilt from evidence rather than from the original promise. | Sections 4 to 6 |

If you can answer all six, and you can get everyone on the same page with them, you have probably got the work back on track. At minimum you are on the track to getting it back on track, which on day ten is the honest version of the claim.

---

## 1. Find out how it is broken, why it is broken, and where it is broken

Three questions, not one. People collapse them and then fix the wrong thing.

- **How** it is broken is the symptom. Dates slip. Requests pile up. Nobody trusts the status.
- **Why** it is broken is the cause. In most cases it's unrealistic expectations held by one or more stakeholders, and everything downstream of it is a consequence rather than a problem in its own right.
- **Where** it's broken is the location, and it's the one people skip. It tells you who to go talk to. A handoff between two teams, a funding decision made a year ago, a request that gets reinterpreted every time it changes hands.

Write down your answer to all three before you propose anything. If you can't answer **why**, you're about to fix a symptom.

### Go one level under "unrealistic expectations"

Expectations do not usually become unrealistic because somebody was being unreasonable. They become unrealistic because the communication was bad, and then two groups spent a year operating on different pictures without either of them finding out.

That means the remedy is not a better plan. It's more communication than feels necessary.

Overcommunicate. Say the same thing to different audiences and check that it actually landed, rather than checking that you said it. **Say it in different ways until everyone gets it**, because the version that lands with an engineering lead is rarely the version that lands with the person paying for the work.

That is also where the recovery starts. Getting everyone hearing the same thing is not preparation for fixing the program. It is the first part of fixing it.

---

## 2. Talk to three groups, and do not skip the third

- **The people doing the work.** They know where it actually breaks.
- **The people asking for the work.** They know what it's for, which isn't always what is being built.
- **The people funding the work.** They decide whether any of it continues, and they're usually the last to be consulted and the first to be surprised.

Meet them separately. A joint kickoff at this stage produces the version of the story everyone is willing to say in front of everyone else, which is the least useful version.

Introduce yourself and explain what you do. This sounds like a formality and it is not. If people don't know what a technical program manager is for, they will assume you're there to audit them, and you'll get audited answers.

---

## 3. Ask each of them the same three questions

Not a requirements interview. You are collecting three different accounts of the same work:

1. What do they **think** is going on?
2. What do they think they are **supposed** to be doing?
3. What do they believe **actually** needs to get done?

Then ask what they are hearing, and what frustrates them.

The value is not in any one answer. It is in the gaps between them. Put every answer into one list across every person you spoke to, and the breakdown will show itself. Then name it:

- Is it resources?
- Is it time?
- Is it budget?
- Is it a communication drop, where something never arrived?
- Is it a miscommunication, where someone said one thing and someone else expected another?

---

## 4. Baseline it, with data

You can't renegotiate anything from an opinion about capacity, because an opinion invites a competing opinion and the louder person wins.

Go back through everything the team has actually completed over the last couple of years. If nobody has sized any of it, size it now. Use three sizes and give them enough spread to be meaningful, for example 5, 8 and 13. If the historical work was never sized, assign it all one middle value. That's crude and it's fine, because what you need out of this is one number: **how much this team can genuinely finish in a sprint.**

That number is the whole negotiation. Without it, you are arguing. With it, you are reading.

A team of five people who can finish roughly forty points each per sprint has a real capacity of about two hundred. If the stakeholders are collectively asking for five hundred, the conversation is no longer about whether people are working hard enough.

---

## 5. Make it visible, upward and downward

Build one view that shows what was completed, how long it took, and what is in flight now. Keep using the same view going forward, so the record of what you promised sits next to the record of what landed.

Visibility upward is reporting, and most people do that part. **Visibility downward is what makes accountability land instead of feeling like surveillance.** The team has to be able to see the same picture their leadership sees. If only one direction can see it, you haven't built transparency, you have built a monitoring system.

Data is what makes the rest of this stick. It's hard to refute a record of what a team actually finished, and once people can't refute it they start lining up behind it.

---

## 6. Negotiate the rebaseline

Not align. Negotiate. Aligning implies the interests already agree, and if they agreed you wouldn't have been called.

Take your capacity number to the people asking for the work, one at a time, and walk their portion of the backlog with them:

> This is two years of requests. Most of it is not going to happen. What matters most right now, and how big is it?

Then give them a real choice inside a real limit. A structure that works:

**Each person on the team takes two large, two medium and two small items per sprint.**

Sizing is by working time, not by importance:

| Size | Roughly |
|---|---|
| Large | About a week of work |
| Medium | Up to about a day |
| Small | Up to about an hour |

Mediums fill in around the larges. Smalls fill in around the mediums. Nobody is ever blocked with nothing to pick up.

With several stakeholders you'll hold more than one large at a time, and that's the point. Three larges and ten mediums in the funnel gives you room to route the right work to the right person without renegotiating anything.

### The cap is an upper limit, not a quota

This is the part that gets read backwards. The cap constrains what you're willing to **promise**, not what the team is allowed to **deliver**. If somebody finishes early they pull more in. That isn't a planning failure, it's upside, and it lands on a stakeholder who is already satisfied rather than on a commitment you missed.

Leave slack in deliberately. A plan that can only be met perfectly is a plan that won't be met.

---

## 7. Hold up your end

The cap is only honest if somebody clears the road.

**If anyone is blocked for more than a day, it comes to you, and you remove the blocker.** That's the trade. You are asking the team to commit to a smaller number, and in exchange the number is real.

This is most of the job in the first ninety days, and it's the part that doesn't appear on any status report.

---

## 8. When emergency work arrives, and it will

Go back to the stakeholders and talk about the trade. You can do this now, and you could not before, because you have three things you did not have: what is getting done, how it's getting done, and evidence that it's getting done.

Something pushes in and something else pushes out. Once the ledger is visible, that stops being an argument and becomes arithmetic. These turn into short, easy conversations, which isn't how they went before.

---

## 9. Expect throughput to rise, and understand why

A team in this situation is usually slowing down, and the instinct is to read that as a discipline problem and apply pressure. That reading is backwards.

Throughput falls when nothing comes back as positive feedback. People stop pushing when pushing has never once been acknowledged. Add demand to that team and the number gets worse.

So do the other thing. Call out the work when it is genuinely good. Say so to the team, and say so to their manager, and say so to their manager's manager, so that people outside the team know what is getting done. It costs nothing and it's usually the first positive signal anyone there has had in months.

Once the rhythm is established, capacity goes up on its own. Rebaseline against the new number rather than quietly pocketing it.

---

## 10. Ask for more only after everything is aligned

If the work genuinely needs more people, more time or more money, that ask comes last.

The instinct in a broken program is to open with it. Do not. An ask made before you have baselined anything sounds like a complaint, and it will be received as one. The same ask made after everyone is looking at the same realistic picture is a conclusion they have already reached themselves, and you're simply the person saying it out loud.

---

## What this looks like on a calendar

It doesn't resolve in a week, and any guide that promises otherwise is selling something.

| By | What is true |
|---|---|
| **Day 1** | You have met the people doing the work, asking for the work, and funding it. |
| **Day 30** | Everyone is thinking about the same set of work and roughly the same idea of what can get done. The arguing is about priorities rather than about reality. |
| **Day 90** | The rhythm holds. Sprints land. Emergency work is being traded rather than absorbed. |
| **Day 180** | The reputation has turned. |

That last one is the actual deliverable, and it's worth being specific about what it means. A team that people did not want to work with becomes a team people describe as successful. Nothing about the engineers changed. What changed is that they were asked for an amount of work they could actually do, and then everyone could see them do it.

---

## The check, at any point

When you are not sure whether the recovery is working, go back to the six questions and ask them of the group rather than of yourself:

- Does everyone give the same answer to **what** we are doing?
- Does everyone give the same answer to **when** it lands?
- Does everyone name the same **who** as owning it?
- Does everyone agree on **why** it went wrong in the first place?

Disagreement on any of those is where the next problem is already forming, and it is cheaper to find it here than in a status meeting two months from now.

---

## What it costs

People will be unhappy at first, and you should expect that rather than be surprised by it. You are telling stakeholders they're going to get less than they asked for. The first two or three sprints are the hard part.

Resistance comes from both directions, which is the part that catches people out. Leadership resists the smaller number. The team resists having a cap at all. Both are reasonable positions and both soften once the work starts landing on time.

You are not going to talk anyone out of either objection. You are going to outlast it with evidence.
